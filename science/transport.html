See it in the P4 figures

  • Celery in red dye: the red water climbs only the water-carrying tubes. Look inside the stem.
  • The ringing experiment: cut away the outer ring with the food-carrying tubes and food piles up above the cut; the leaves stay green because the water-carrying tubes are still there.
Question: A ring of bark is removed from a tree. Why does the part above the ring swell, but the leaves stay green?

The food-carrying tubes in the outer layer are removed, so food from the leaves cannot travel down past the ring and collects above it. The water-carrying tubes inside are not cut, so water still reaches the leaves.

Transpiration and stomata

2. Water lost from the leaves

Open full page

Key ideas

  • Stomata are tiny openings, mainly on the underside of leaves.
  • They are used for gaseous exchange (carbon dioxide in, oxygen out) and they let water vapour out.
  • Losing water vapour from the leaves is called transpiration. It helps draw water up the stem from the roots.
  • Warm, sunny or windy weather → more water lost.
  • Very hot and dry → the stomata close to prevent too much water loss (and wilting).

How the experiment works

  • The oil layer stops water evaporating from the cylinder, so any drop in water level is water taken up by the plant.
  • Grease on the undersides blocks the stomata: much less water is lost, which shows most stomata are on the underside.
  • A plastic bag over the leaves collects droplets: the water vapour from the leaves condenses on the cooler bag.

Try it

  • Wait 1 hour in each kind of weather and compare how much the level drops. Then choose “very hot and dry” and look under a leaf. Grease the undersides and wait again.
Question: Why is a layer of oil added on top of the water?

To prevent water from evaporating from the surface of the water, so that the drop in water level is only due to water taken in by the plant.

Exam skills

5. Practice questions

Practice 1: Describe how water in the soil reaches the leaves of a plant.

Water is absorbed by the root hairs and roots. It travels up the water-carrying tubes in the stem to the leaves.

Practice 2: Two identical plants are left in the sun. Plant A is in a windy place; plant B is in a still place. Which loses more water? Explain.

Plant A. The wind carries water vapour away from the leaves, so water evaporates and escapes through the stomata faster.

Practice 3: Grease is applied to the underside of the leaves of plant X and to the upper side of the leaves of plant Y. Which plant loses less water? Why?

Plant X. Most stomata are on the underside of the leaves; grease blocks them, so less water vapour can escape.

Practice 4: On a very hot, dry afternoon, the stomata of a plant close. How does this help the plant?

Closing the stomata reduces water loss through transpiration, so the plant does not lose too much water and wilt.

Practice 5: Give one way the transport system of a plant is different from the circulatory system of a human.

A human has a heart that pumps blood in a continuous loop, but a plant has no heart: water moves only upward, drawn by absorption and transpiration, and is lost from the leaves.
